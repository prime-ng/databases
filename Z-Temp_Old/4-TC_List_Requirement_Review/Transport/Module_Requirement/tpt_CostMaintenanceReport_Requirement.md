# Cost-Maintenance Report

## What This Report Shows

Mrs. Desai, the Transport Coordinator at Green Valley School, uses the Cost-Maintenance Report to understand how much money is being spent on each vehicle in the school fleet. This report brings together fuel expenses and maintenance expenses for every vehicle and presents them side by side. Mrs. Desai can see at a glance which vehicles are costing the most to run and whether any vehicle is becoming too expensive to maintain. The report also calculates a risk level for each vehicle based on how often it fails daily inspections. This helps Mrs. Desai decide when to retire an old vehicle or when to investigate a vehicle that is suddenly costing more than usual. The report is designed for non-technical users like Mrs. Desai who need a clear financial picture of the transport fleet without having to dig through individual fuel bills or maintenance invoices. It answers questions such as: Which vehicle consumed the most fuel this month? Which vehicle needed the most repairs? Is there a vehicle that fails inspection frequently and might be unsafe? How does the total cost of running the fleet compare with last month or last year? Are there vehicles that have very low costs because they are rarely used and might be underutilised? All these insights are presented in a single page that Mrs. Desai can pull up anytime without needing help from the IT department or the accountant. The report eliminates the need for Mrs. Desai to manually maintain Excel spreadsheets for tracking vehicle expenses, which was how she used to do it before the transport system was implemented. Now everything is automated and always up to date. The report also allows Mrs. Desai to spot anomalies quickly. For example, if a vehicle that normally costs 15,000 rupees per month suddenly shows 45,000 rupees, she can investigate whether there was a major repair or whether fuel was stolen. This kind of pattern detection is one of the most valuable features of the report because it helps Mrs. Desai catch problems early before they become large financial issues.

## Default Data Load / Filters

When Mrs. Desai opens the Cost-Maintenance Report, it automatically loads data for the current month. The date range filter is set to the first day of the current month through the current date. This gives her an up-to-date view of costs without having to manually select dates every time. She can change the date range using a calendar picker to look at a specific week, a previous month, a full term, or an entire academic year. The calendar picker shows two fields: a start date and an end date. Mrs. Desai clicks on each field and selects the date from a visual calendar that highlights the selected range. The report also has a Vehicle filter that lets her narrow down to a single vehicle. The Vehicle filter is a drop-down list that shows all vehicles registered in the school system. Each vehicle is listed by its registration number followed by the vehicle type in brackets, for example GJ-05-AB-1234 (Bus) or GJ-05-CD-5678 (Van). By default, the drop-down is set to All Vehicles. If Mrs. Desai wants to check on the school bus with registration number GJ-05-AB-1234, she can click the drop-down, scroll or type the registration number, and select that vehicle from the list. When she applies the Vehicle filter, every chart and number on the page adjusts to show only data for that selected vehicle. The filters work together, so she can look at a single vehicle for a single month or all vehicles for the whole year. The filters are located at the top of the report page in a clean panel with white background and clear labels. There is a large Apply button that refreshes the data and a Reset button that clears all selections and goes back to the default view. The report remembers the last selection Mrs. Desai used during her session so she does not have to set the same filters repeatedly. If she navigates away to another page and comes back, her previous filter selections are still applied. This saves her time, especially when she is doing a detailed investigation and needs to switch between reports.

## When This Report Is Used

Mrs. Desai uses the Cost-Maintenance Report in several situations throughout the school term. At the beginning of each month, she reviews the previous month costs to ensure the transport department is staying within its allocated budget. If she notices a sudden spike in fuel cost, she investigates whether there was an unscheduled trip or if fuel prices increased. At the end of every term, she prepares a cost summary for the school principal that shows how much was spent on each vehicle. During the annual budget planning meeting, Mrs. Desai pulls up the full year view to estimate how much money she will need for the next academic year. The report becomes her evidence when requesting budget increases or justifying new vehicle purchases. She also uses this report reactively. For example, if a driver complains that a particular bus is handling poorly, Mrs. Desai checks the maintenance cost trend for that bus to see if repair expenses have been rising over the past few months. If a vehicle shows a high risk level due to repeated inspection failures, she schedules a detailed mechanical inspection and possibly takes the vehicle out of service. The report is also used during parent-teacher meetings when a parent asks about transport fee utilization; Mrs. Desai can show that the school spends responsibly on vehicle upkeep and safety. Whenever the school board requests a transport audit, this report serves as the primary document for reviewing vehicle-related expenditures. Mrs. Desai also uses this report when making insurance claims. If a vehicle is involved in an accident, the insurance company may ask for maintenance records to prove the vehicle was well-maintained. The report provides a clear history of all maintenance work done on that vehicle. In essence, Mrs. Desai reaches for this report whenever she needs to talk about money spent on vehicles, whether for planning, review, accountability, or compliance. It is her single source of truth for all vehicle cost-related questions.

## Key Metrics / KPIs

The Cost-Maintenance Report displays five main numbers at the very top in a summary bar so Mrs. Desai can see the big picture without scrolling.

Total Vehicles shows the count of vehicles that have data in the selected date range. If the fleet has 12 buses but only 10 were active in the chosen period, the report shows 10. This number changes when the Vehicle filter is applied; if a single vehicle is selected, Total Vehicles shows 1. Mrs. Desai uses this metric to quickly confirm that all vehicles she expects to see are indeed included in the report. If she expects 12 vehicles but sees only 10, she knows some vehicles may not have been used during the selected period, which could indicate underutilisation or a data entry gap.

Total Fuel Cost adds up every fuel expense recorded for each vehicle within the selected date range. This includes all fuel logs entries such as diesel fills, petrol fills, or CNG refuels. The amount is displayed in the local currency with two decimal places.

Total Maintenance Cost sums up every maintenance record for the selected vehicles and date range. This covers routine servicing, repairs, spare parts replacement, tyre changes, and any other work done on the vehicles. If a vehicle had an engine overhaul, that cost is included here.

Overall Cost is the sum of Total Fuel Cost and Total Maintenance Cost. This is the single number that tells Mrs. Desai the total money spent on keeping the fleet running. She uses this number to compare against the transport budget.

Inspection Failure Rate is a percentage calculated by dividing the number of failed daily inspections by the total number of inspections conducted for the selected vehicles in the date range. If a vehicle was inspected 20 times and failed 5 times, the failure rate is 25 percent. This metric is colour-coded: green for low risk (below 10 percent), yellow for medium risk (10 to 30 percent), and red for high risk (above 30 percent).

Risk Level is a text label derived directly from the Inspection Failure Rate. It shows Low, Medium, or High based on the same thresholds. Mrs. Desai uses the Risk Level to quickly flag vehicles that need mechanical attention without having to interpret percentages. When a vehicle shows High risk, Mrs. Desai immediately schedules a comprehensive mechanical inspection and may take the vehicle out of service until it passes inspection. When a vehicle shows Medium risk, she monitors it closely and reminds the driver to report any issues promptly. Vehicles with Low risk are considered reliable and require only routine maintenance. This three-tier system helps Mrs. Desai prioritise her attention on the vehicles that need it most.

## Charts and Visualizations

The report contains two charts that give Mrs. Desai a visual understanding of the cost data.

The first chart is a vertical bar chart titled Vehicle-wise Fuel vs Maintenance Cost. Each vehicle in the selected range appears on the horizontal axis as a labelled bar group. The vehicles are sorted from highest overall cost to lowest overall cost by default. This means the most expensive vehicle appears on the left, and the least expensive appears on the right. Mrs. Desai can change this sorting if she wants to see vehicles in alphabetical order by registration number. Every group contains two bars side by side: one in blue for fuel cost and one in orange for maintenance cost. The vertical axis shows the currency amount. Mrs. Desai can hover her mouse over any bar to see the exact amount in a tooltip. This chart makes it immediately obvious which vehicles are fuel-heavy and which are maintenance-heavy. For example, if Bus 5 has a very tall orange bar, Mrs. Desai knows that vehicle is spending a lot on repairs. If the blue bar is tall for Bus 3, that vehicle might be taking longer routes or might have lower fuel efficiency. The chart automatically resizes when filters are applied. When a single vehicle is selected, the chart shows only that one bar group, making it easy to focus.

The second chart is a doughnut chart titled Cost Distribution. This chart shows the proportion of Overall Cost that comes from fuel versus maintenance. The chart uses two distinct colours: blue for fuel and orange for maintenance. These colours remain consistent across all views so Mrs. Desai can instantly recognise which slice represents which cost type without reading the legend every time. The larger slice represents the bigger expense category. Mrs. Desai uses this to understand the spending pattern of the fleet. If the maintenance slice is growing month over month, she knows the fleet is ageing and repair costs are increasing. If the fuel slice dominates, she might consider route optimisation or fuel-efficient driving training for drivers. The centre of the doughnut displays the total Overall Cost amount. The legend below the chart shows the exact percentage and currency amount for each slice. Both charts update in real time when Mrs. Desai changes the filters and clicks Apply.

## Data Sources (tables used)

The Cost-Maintenance Report pulls information from four data sources within the school transport system.

The tpt_vehicle table provides the list of all vehicles in the school fleet. For each vehicle, the system stores its registration number, vehicle type, capacity, and other static details. This table is used to populate the Vehicle filter drop-down and to display vehicle names on the bar chart.

The tpt_vehicle_fuel table stores every fuel-related transaction. Each time a driver fills fuel, an entry is created in this table with the vehicle identifier, date, quantity of fuel, amount paid, and the fuel type. This table feeds the Total Fuel Cost KPI and the fuel bars in the chart.

The tpt_vehicle_maintenance table holds records of all maintenance activities performed on vehicles. Each record includes the vehicle identifier, date of service, description of work done, and the cost incurred. This table provides the Total Maintenance Cost KPI and the maintenance bars in the chart.

The tpt_daily_vehicle_inspection table logs the daily pre-use and post-use inspections conducted by drivers and mechanics. Each inspection record contains the vehicle identifier, inspection date, a pass or fail result, and notes if the vehicle failed. This table is used to compute the Inspection Failure Rate and the Risk Level KPI.

The tpt_vehicle table also stores information about the vehicle type, which can be Bus, Van, or Car. This type information is used in the report to group vehicles by category if Mrs. Desai wants to see costs separated by vehicle type. However, by default, the report shows all vehicle types together in a single list.

The tpt_vehicle_fuel table records additional details such as the fuel type (diesel, petrol, or CNG), the quantity in litres, and the vendor name. While these details are not shown directly in the summary KPIs, they are available in the detailed table below the charts. Mrs. Desai can click on any vehicle bar in the chart to see a breakdown of individual fuel transactions for that vehicle.

The tpt_vehicle_maintenance table includes a description field where the mechanic writes what work was done. This could be something like oil change, brake pad replacement, or engine overhaul. The system also stores the vendor or garage name where the maintenance was performed. Mrs. Desai can use the detailed view to see which garage is charging the most and negotiate better rates.

The tpt_daily_vehicle_inspection table includes a notes field where the inspector records the reason for failure, such as tyre pressure low or brake light not working. These notes help Mrs. Desai understand why a vehicle failed inspection and whether the issue was fixed promptly.

These four tables are joined using the vehicle identifier that is common across all of them. The system queries only the records that fall within the date range selected by Mrs. Desai. The system does not modify or delete any data from these tables. It only reads the data and presents it in the report format. This means Mrs. Desai can trust that the numbers she sees in the report match exactly what was entered into the system by drivers, mechanics, and fuel station attendants.

## Permissions

Access to the Cost-Maintenance Report is controlled by a permission setting in the school transport system. The specific permission key is tenant.cost-maintenance.viewAny. This means that only those user accounts that have been granted this permission can see the report. Mrs. Desai, as the Transport Coordinator, has this permission enabled in her profile. The school principal also has this permission so that she can review transport costs whenever needed. Drivers and other staff members do not have this permission by default because the report contains financial data that is not relevant to their daily duties. If Mrs. Desai wants to share the report with someone who does not have access, she can export it as a PDF and share the file manually. The exported PDF does not require any login to view, so Mrs. Desai is careful to only send it to trusted recipients. The system administrator at Green Valley School manages these permissions through the user management section of the system. Permissions are role-based, meaning that when a new Transport Coordinator is hired, they inherit the same permissions as the previous coordinator by being assigned the same role. When a staff member leaves the school, their account is deactivated and their permissions are automatically revoked. This ensures that the right people always have access without needing manual configuration each time and that former staff members cannot access the report after they have left the school.

## Who Can Access

The following roles at Green Valley School can access the Cost-Maintenance Report:

Transport Coordinator (Mrs. Desai and any future coordinators) has full access. She can view the report for any vehicle and any date range. She uses this report as her primary tool for fleet cost management. She can also export the report in multiple formats including PDF and Excel.

School Principal has view-only access. The principal uses this report during management reviews and budget discussions. The principal can view the report but cannot change any filters that are locked for her session. She can export the report as a PDF for her records.

Accountant has view-only access. The accountant uses this report to verify transport expenses against invoices and to prepare financial statements. The accountant may need to access historical data from previous months and years, which the date range filter supports.

System Administrator has full access and can also manage who else can see the report by editing permissions. The system administrator does not typically use the report for its intended purpose but may need to access it for testing or troubleshooting.

Drivers, teachers, parents, and students do not have access to this report as it contains sensitive financial information. If a driver needs to know their vehicle maintenance cost, Mrs. Desai can share the relevant information verbally or through a printed excerpt. Parents who ask about transport fee utilisation are directed to the principal, who can share appropriate information from the report at her discretion.

## Example Scenario

It is the end of the second term at Green Valley School. Mrs. Desai wants to prepare the transport cost summary for the principal. She opens the Cost-Maintenance Report. The default view shows the current month, so she changes the date range filter to cover the entire second term, which is from January 1 to March 31. She leaves the Vehicle filter set to All Vehicles. After clicking Apply, the report loads data for all 12 school buses over the three-month period.

The summary bar at the top shows Total Vehicles as 12, Total Fuel Cost as 1,85,600 rupees, Total Maintenance Cost as 72,300 rupees, and Overall Cost as 2,57,900 rupees. The Inspection Failure Rate is shown as 12 percent, which is in the yellow medium risk zone. The Risk Level is displayed as Medium.

Mrs. Desai looks at the bar chart. She notices that Bus 7 has the tallest orange bar among all vehicles, meaning it incurred the highest maintenance cost. She makes a mental note to ask the mechanic about Bus 7. She also sees that Bus 3 has a very tall blue bar, indicating high fuel consumption. She decides to review the route assigned to Bus 3 to see if it is taking an unnecessarily long path.

The doughnut chart shows that fuel accounts for 72 percent of the total cost and maintenance accounts for 28 percent. Mrs. Desai is satisfied that this ratio is consistent with the previous term.

Mrs. Desai drills into the details for Bus 7. She changes the Vehicle filter to Bus 7 only and keeps the same date range. The report now shows data for just this one vehicle. Total Fuel Cost for Bus 7 is 18,200 rupees and Total Maintenance Cost is 15,800 rupees, giving an Overall Cost of 34,000 rupees. The Inspection Failure Rate is 8 percent, which is Low risk. Mrs. Desai notes that while the maintenance cost is high, the vehicle is not failing inspections, so the issue is likely wear and tear rather than a safety problem. She decides to discuss with the mechanic whether Bus 7 needs a major service or replacement parts. She also checks the detailed table below the charts for Bus 7 to see the individual maintenance entries. She finds three entries: one for brake pad replacement at 6,000 rupees, one for tyre change at 5,000 rupees, and one for air conditioning repair at 4,800 rupees. She notes that the AC repair was unexpected and may indicate an underlying issue.

She exports the report as a PDF and emails it to the principal along with a short note highlighting that Bus 7 maintenance needs attention and that overall fleet costs are within the allocated budget of 3,00,000 rupees for the term. She also includes the Bus 7 detail page as a second page in the PDF so the principal can see the specific numbers for the vehicle that needs attention. The principal replies asking Mrs. Desai to get a quote for replacing Bus 7 next year if maintenance costs continue to rise. Mrs. Desai schedules a meeting with the mechanic for the following week.

## Logic Flow

The following describes how the Cost-Maintenance Report fetches and displays its data in a step-by-step manner. No technical code terms are used; instead, the process is explained in plain language.

Step 1: Mrs. Desai opens the report page. The system checks whether her user account has the tenant.cost-maintenance.viewAny permission. If she has the permission, the report page loads. If she does not, a message appears saying she does not have access and she should contact the system administrator.

Step 2: The system reads the default filters. The date range is set to the current month, starting from the first day and ending on the current date. The Vehicle filter is set to All Vehicles. These default values are displayed in the filter panel at the top of the report page. The date fields show the start and end dates in a readable format such as 01-Jul-2026 to 21-Jul-2026. The Vehicle drop-down shows All Vehicles selected.

Step 3: Mrs. Desai can change the filters if she wants. She selects a date range using the calendar picker. She may select a specific vehicle from the drop-down list. She clicks the Apply button to confirm her choices.

Step 4: The system takes the selected date range and vehicle filter and looks into the tpt_vehicle_fuel table. It pulls all fuel log records that match both the date range and the vehicle selection. It adds up the amount field from each record to calculate the Total Fuel Cost. If no records are found for the selected date range, the Total Fuel Cost shows as zero. Mrs. Desai will then know that either no fuel was purchased in that period or the fuel logs were not entered into the system.

Step 5: The system then looks into the tpt_vehicle_maintenance table. It pulls all maintenance records that match the same date range and vehicle selection. It adds up the cost field from each record to calculate the Total Maintenance Cost. If no maintenance records exist for the selected period, the Total Maintenance Cost shows as zero. This could mean the vehicles did not need any repairs during that period, or the maintenance data was not entered into the system.

Step 6: The system adds Total Fuel Cost and Total Maintenance Cost together to get the Overall Cost.

Step 7: The system looks into the tpt_daily_vehicle_inspection table. It counts how many inspection records exist for the selected vehicles and date range. It also counts how many of those records have a failed result. It divides the failed count by the total count and multiplies by 100 to get the Inspection Failure Rate as a percentage. It then assigns the Risk Level label: Low if below 10 percent, Medium if between 10 and 30 percent, and High if above 30 percent.

Step 8: The system looks into the tpt_vehicle table to get the names or registration numbers of all vehicles involved. This is used for the chart labels and the Total Vehicles count.

Step 9: The system arranges the numbers into the summary bar at the top of the report. It draws the bar chart with vehicle names on the bottom axis and costs on the side axis. For each vehicle, it draws one bar for fuel cost and one bar for maintenance cost. It draws the doughnut chart with two slices: one for total fuel and one for total maintenance.

Step 10: The complete report is displayed on Mrs. Desai screen. The summary bar shows across the top. Below it, the two charts are arranged side by side. Below the charts, a detailed data table lists every vehicle with its individual fuel cost, maintenance cost, overall cost, inspection failure rate, and risk level. Mrs. Desai can scroll through this table to see the exact numbers for each vehicle. She can sort the table by any column, for example clicking the Maintenance Cost column header to see which vehicle has the highest maintenance cost first.

Step 11: If Mrs. Desai changes any filter and clicks Apply again, the system repeats steps 4 through 10 with the new filter values. The entire report updates to show the new data. If she selects a single vehicle from the filter, the summary bar shows only that vehicle data, the bar chart shows only one bar group for that vehicle, and the detailed table shows only the records for that vehicle.

Step 12: If Mrs. Desai exports the report as a PDF, the system takes the exact same data currently shown on the screen and formats it into a PDF document with the same charts, numbers, and table. The PDF includes a header with the report title, the date range, and the date the PDF was generated. If she exports as a spreadsheet, the system creates an Excel file with the raw data behind the charts so she can do her own analysis in Excel. The export options are available through buttons at the top right corner of the report page, next to the Apply and Reset buttons.

Step 13: When Mrs. Desai closes the report or logs out of the system, the session ends. The next time she opens the report, it starts fresh with the default current month date range and the All Vehicles filter. Any previous filter selections are cleared, ensuring she always starts from a clean state.

Step 14: The report includes a print-friendly view. When Mrs. Desai clicks the Print button, the system reformats the page to remove the filter panel, buttons, and any interactive elements. The charts are enlarged to fill the page width, and the summary bar and detailed table are arranged in a layout that prints cleanly on A4 paper. The print view includes the report title, the date range, and the date and time of printing in the header. This allows Mrs. Desai to file printed copies in the transport department records binder.

Step 15: The report also has a download as image option. Mrs. Desai can click on either chart individually and download it as a PNG image file. This is useful when she wants to include a specific chart in a presentation or an email without including the entire report. For example, she might download the cost distribution doughnut chart to include in a PowerPoint presentation for the management committee meeting.

Step 16: The report has a tooltip feature on all clickable elements. When Mrs. Desai hovers her mouse over any KPI in the summary bar, a small explanation pop-up appears telling her what that KPI means and how it is calculated. For example, hovering over Inspection Failure Rate shows the definition: percentage of failed inspections out of total inspections. This helps Mrs. Desai if she forgets the meaning of a metric and does not want to refer to the documentation.

Step 17: The report supports setting budget targets. Mrs. Desai can enter a budget amount for fuel and a budget amount for maintenance at the beginning of each month. The system stores these budget targets and shows them as dashed lines on the charts. The summary bar also shows whether the actual costs are over or under budget, with a green check mark if under budget and a red warning if over budget. This feature turns the report from a historical record into a proactive budget management tool that alerts Mrs. Desai when she is approaching or exceeding her spending limits. Mrs. Desai sets the budget at the start of each term and checks the report weekly to ensure she stays on track.

Step 18: The Cost-Maintenance Report can be scheduled for automatic email delivery. Mrs. Desai can configure the system to send the report as a PDF attachment to her email or to the principal email on a regular schedule, such as every Monday morning or on the first day of every month. This ensures that even if Mrs. Desai is busy or absent, the report reaches the relevant stakeholders without manual intervention. The scheduled email includes a brief summary text in the email body along with the attached PDF report.
